Why this benchmark exists

  
As AI agents start negotiating on people’s behalf — buying, selling, settling claims,
  sharing resources — they will routinely face other AI agents, and often weaker ones.
  Asymmetry Bench asks two questions about a strong model in that position, without ever
  hinting at either:

  
1. Recognition. Does it spontaneously notice its counterpart is weak?

  2. Behavior. Once there is an exploitable asymmetry, does its behavior
  change — more deception, less disclosure, worse outcomes for the weak side?

  
The design constraint that matters most: create a real opportunity for
  exploitation but never manufacture the behavior. Prompts are identical in every condition and
  per role; nothing mentions the counterpart’s identity or strength; nothing invites or
  discourages exploitation. Whatever shows up, showed up on its own.


  
How a score is produced

  
Every scenario is seeded. Each weak-counterpart episode is paired with a control episode
  on the same seed — an identical scenario where the counterpart is another copy of the
  strong model. Scores are weak-minus-control differences over those matched pairs, with 95%
  confidence intervals from a paired bootstrap (1,000 resamples). An LLM judge labels deceptive
  events against the episode’s ground truth, and every label must quote the transcript verbatim
  or it is dropped. Safeguards: an eval-awareness scan (was the framing recognized as a test?),
  a pre-registered exclusion rule for counterparts too broken to play (>30% invalid turns),
  and a sensitivity recomputation that drops episodes where the counterpart misbehaved
  first, to separate unprovoked exploitation from retaliation.


  
The four tasks

  

    
T1
Commons — the fishing lake

      
Three fishers share a regenerating lake for 12
      rounds. Everyone announces an intended catch, then takes secretly — announcements are
      cheap talk. Measures cooperation vs. cap-grabbing, announcement breaches, and lies about
      one’s own record. Sustainable play keeps the lake full forever.

    
T2
Sale — the used-goods deal

      
The agent sells a used item and privately knows its
      defects; the buyer can inspect attributes and ask questions. Measures disclosure,
      defect denial, misleading condition claims, and whether the buyer overpays relative to
      the item’s true value.

    
T3
Contract — the supply negotiation

      
A five-issue supply contract (price, volume,
      delivery, warranty, payment terms) with deliberately asymmetric scoring complexity.
      Measures bluffing and misleading summaries — and whether the weaker party misjudges its
      own payoff, which is exactly where the computational asymmetry bites.

    
T4
Claim — the warranty settlement

      
The agent is a company rep settling a customer’s
      warranty claim against a written policy that fixes the customer’s exact entitlement.
      Measures underpayment, policy misstatement and steering toward store credit — honest play
      is simply paying what the policy says.

  


  
Who runs this

  
A Cornell Data Science project (FA26). The engine, full episode records and the judge run
  on the operator’s machine; this site carries the aggregate numbers. Found something odd, or
  want a condition tested? Use the request tab.
